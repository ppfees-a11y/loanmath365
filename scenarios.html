Real-world loan decision scenarios

Understand how the same loan can look affordable by payment but costly in total, and why fees and extra payments matter.

Scenario A: A lower payment with a longer term

Suppose a borrower needs $20,000 at a fixed 9% nominal annual rate. At 36 months, the estimated payment is about $636 and total interest about $2,896. At 60 months, payment falls to about $415 but total interest rises to about $4,910. The smaller payment comes with roughly $2,014 more interest. These rounded illustrations assume monthly amortization and no fees.

Scenario B: A fee reduces the cash you receive

A borrower signs for $10,000 with a $500 origination fee deducted from proceeds. The borrower receives $9,500 but may still owe principal and interest based on $10,000. A separate fee paid in cash has a different immediate cash-flow effect. Ask the lender how the fee is charged and consult the official APR disclosure.

Scenario C: Paying more can shorten repayment

A borrower makes the required payment plus an extra $50 every month. If the lender applies the extra amount immediately to principal and there is no prepayment penalty, the balance generally falls sooner and future interest can decline. The exact benefit depends on the rate, remaining term, payment timing, and the lender’s method. Use the extra-payment planner for an estimate.

Scenario D: An offer with a lower rate may still cost more

Offer A has a lower stated interest rate but a substantial origination fee. Offer B has a slightly higher rate and no fee. Depending on loan size and term, either could have a lower overall cost. Compare the lender-disclosed APR, total payments, and cash proceeds, not a rate headline alone.

Scenario E: Debt-to-income is not disposable income

Gross monthly income is $5,000 and existing required debt payments are $1,000. A proposed $400 payment produces an estimated 28% DTI: ($1,000 + $400) / $5,000. That does not mean the borrower has $3,600 left to spend: taxes, housing costs not counted as debt, groceries, utilities, and savings still matter.

How to use these examples

Start on the calculators page and enter your own proposed loan amount, rate, and term. Compare the results against the lender’s official disclosures. All scenarios are hypothetical, not recommendations or predictions of approval.
