How a Loan Payment Is Calculated

For a typical fixed-rate installment loan, each monthly payment is divided between interest and principal. Early in the schedule, a larger share may go to interest because the outstanding balance is higher. As principal is repaid, the interest portion generally falls.

What the Interest Rate Changes

With the same loan amount and term, a higher interest rate generally produces a higher monthly payment and more total interest. Even a seemingly small rate difference can matter when the balance is large or the repayment period is long.

What the Loan Term Changes

A longer term can reduce the required monthly payment because repayment is spread over more months. However, keeping a balance outstanding longer can increase total interest. A shorter term generally increases the monthly payment while reducing the number of interest-bearing months.

Extra Principal Payments

When a lender applies extra payments directly to principal and there is no prepayment penalty, reducing the balance faster can shorten payoff time and reduce future interest. Loan contracts and lender procedures differ, so borrowers should verify how an extra payment will be applied.

Understanding the Results

Monthly Payment

The scheduled principal-and-interest amount under the assumptions entered. It does not automatically include origination fees, insurance, taxes, or other charges.

Total Interest

The estimated sum of interest paid over the scheduled term if payments are made as modeled.

Total Repayment

The estimated principal plus interest paid over the modeled repayment period. This is not necessarily the same as a lender's disclosed total cost when other charges apply.

APR vs. Interest Rate

The stated interest rate is used to calculate interest on the balance. Annual percentage rate (APR) is a broader disclosure measure that can incorporate certain finance charges. The two figures therefore may not be identical.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does this calculator work for mortgages?

The basic amortization math can estimate principal and interest on many fixed-rate loans, but a mortgage payment can also include property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, association fees, or other costs. A mortgage-specific calculator is better when those expenses matter.

Does this calculator include loan fees?

No. The calculator models the entered principal, rate, term, and optional extra monthly payment. Origination fees and other charges are not included.

What happens with a 0% interest loan?

The principal is divided evenly across the selected number of monthly payments.

Can an extra payment save interest?

It can when the extra amount reduces principal earlier, but the actual effect depends on the loan agreement and how the lender applies payments.

Is APR the same as my interest rate?

Not necessarily. APR can reflect certain additional finance charges and is intended to help compare borrowing costs.

Why might my lender quote a different payment?

Differences can result from fees, payment dates, rounding methods, compounding conventions, insurance, taxes, adjustable rates, or other contract terms.
